The headline is stark. And yes, Europe’s automotive industry is under enormous pressure.

But I do not believe that fatalism will help us move forward. In the public debate, it is understandable that we focus on tangible, emotionally charged terms such as the “Verbrennerverbot,” or place our hopes in concepts such as “hocheffiziente Verbrenner,” a phrase taken up a little while ago in Chancellor Friedrich Merz’s letter to European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen. 🦾 Technology choices matter. But they cannot solve the economics of an underutilized manufacturing footprint. Many European vehicle plants are operating at around 70% capacity, or even less. In a business with automotive margins, that is simply not a healthy operating model. This is the difficult reality behind today’s conversations about consolidating plants, employment and supplier structures. Behind these numbers are people, families, communities and often generations of industrial experience. We should never discuss restructuring as if it were an abstract exercise. But that is also why we owe everyone involved an honest conversation. Delaying difficult decisions may feel easier today, but it leaves us with less time and less capital to shape the transition responsibly. 🚀 Consolidation is not the objective. Renewal is. Europe needs the capacity to invest in affordable electric vehicles, software, faster development cycles and products that customers genuinely want. This is even more important now that China is no longer the dependable profit engine it once was, but the world’s most demanding automotive market. Europe has excellent engineers, strong brands and an extraordinary industrial base. Our manufacturers have credible answers. What matters now is executing them with speed, focus and determination. As I told Süddeutsche Zeitung, Germany and Europe must remain “hungry” and resist falling into paralysis when confronted with multiple crises. For me, this is not a message of decline. It is a message of responsibility, urgency and confidence grounded in reality. Thank you to Paulina Würminghausen and Süddeutsche Zeitung for the conversation.

Germany’s most credible AI advantage may not be another chatbot.

It may be the machine that sees, plans, and acts, in the real world. I recently joined Nils Wigger on Business Punk’s “How to Change” to discuss the industrial world now taking shape, and why decline is not a strategy. We explored: → China as a benchmark, not a blueprint: impressive speed, ambition and industrial focus, alongside very real demographic, employment and property challenges. → Germany and Europe have not forgotten how to engineer. Our strength remains the integration of mechanics, electronics, software and safety into complex cyber-physical systems. → Physical AI is where these capabilities meet the next AI wave: robots and autonomous systems that perceive, plan and act. But intelligence alone does not make a reliable industrial robot. The bridge from AI model to real machine requires the full engineering stack: sensor technology, actuators, mechanics, control engineering, functional safety, systems integration, and deep industrial know-how. Germany has considerable strength across this entire stack. The German skill Regelungstechnik (TUM, KIT, Darmstadt, ...) is, for instance, not the sole foundation of robotics, but it is one of the essential disciplines that turns intelligence into controlled, precise, and reliable action. It keeps aircraft in the air, cars on course and robots on their feet. “Make Regelungstechnik great again,” as we put it, is a synonym of a new "lust" for real engineering. More broadly, we need to make these demanding, engineering-heavy capabilities attractive again. To students, talent, investors and society. Because the next industrial wave will not be won by algorithms alone. → Sovereignty does not mean isolation. We need reliable access to AI models, compute, chips and industrial software, while using strong partnerships intelligently. → Above all, technological capability needs long-term investment, implementation speed and a shared appetite for the future: Zukunftslust. A nod to Prof. Alois Knoll, whose decades of work in robotics, AI and real-time systems show that this is not a new German capability, only one we need to rediscover and scale. And Prof. Achim Kampker: your book “Zukunftslust” made it safely into the episode. With “Zukunftsfrust – Zukunftsmut” now freshly published, the timing was almost suspiciously good. 😉 Links in the comments. Thank you, Nils, for a lively, thoughtful and thoroughly enjoyable conversation. My takeaway: Europe has enough knowledge and industrial substance to be a driver, not merely a passenger. But potential only matters when we turn it into action. 🎧 Listen here: So in the spirit of this podcast episode: Listen. Think. Act. The new space race is not about the most spectacular flight.

It is about the ability to build dense satellite networks frequently, and at predictable cost. Jörn Petring examines the first flight of China's Long March 10B on July 10 for WirtschaftsWoche. Roughly six minutes after stage separation, the first stage came back vertically and was caught by a cable system on the marine platform "Linghangzhe" in the South China Sea. With the controlled return of a first stage after an orbital launch, China joins a very exclusive club alongside SpaceX and Blue Origin. The gap remains wide: SpaceX landed its first Falcon 9 stage in 2015, reflew it in 2017, and today stands at more than 600 landings and around 150 launches a year. What matters is the purpose. According to manufacturer CASC, the Long March 10B carries up to 16 metric tons to low Earth orbit (LEO) in its reusable configuration. Ideally sized for satellite internet constellations and large commercial satellites. Satellite networks are strategic infrastructure, and for major powers a question of technological sovereignty. My line in the article: recovered is not reused. A reflight of the same stage has been announced before year end. It would be decisive evidence for the reuse chain, but still not a business model. What counts after that: parts replaced, inspection time, preparation cost, launch cadence. The work at sea belongs in that calculation too: eight ships supported the first recovery. China's possible advantage lies in its industrial system: state groups, private and state-adjacent players developing in parallel, with the 2026–2030 five-year plan naming reusable launchers as an explicit state goal. China's two LEO constellations Guowang ("National Network") and Qianfan ("Thousand Sails") are planned at some 28k satellites. Steady demand! The pattern is familiar. In solar modules, batteries, China combined speed, scale, falling costs and domestic demand. My hypothesis: unlike chips, where China cannot freely source the most advanced lithography systems, no comparable external bottleneck is visible for rocket stages. Matthias Feix of Argon & Co. looks at the Chinese market: still fragmented, several serious candidates side by side, no market leader comparable to SpaceX. If China makes the step to economical reflight, pressure builds for Europe. Not only on price, above all on launch cadence. Ariane 6 secures heavy-lift access but does not bring its main stage back. With Themis, Prometheus and the MaiaSpace launcher, Europe is developing reuse technologies; repeatable flight operations are still ahead of us. What will decide this is how quickly Europe turns technology programs into a system of launcher, series production, secured demand and repeatable operations. "Not whether a technology works, but when it becomes worth using."

As a research, technology and innovation person, this wakes me up! That question may sound like a small distinction. In a fab, it is everything. Journalist Joachim Hofer explores precisely this tension in “Too Good for the World Market Leader” in today’s Handelsblatt print edition. The technology is High-NA, the next generation of EUV lithography. Manifested in a machine that is second to none. Lithography is the step in chipmaking that projects extremely small circuit patterns onto a light-sensitive layer on a silicon wafer. High-NA (Numerical Aperture) uses more advanced optics (put very simply.) to achieve even finer patterns. The hesitant customer named in the article is TSMC, arguably the company best placed to judge its manufacturing value. Hofer reports that TSMC intends to manufacture without High-NA until at least 2029. TSMC Deputy Co-Chief Operating Officer Kevin Zhang described ASML’s latest system as “very, very expensive.” A High-NA machine costs close to €400 million. Approximately twice as much as a current conventional EUV system, according to the article. But the purchase price is only the beginning. TSMC already has highly developed EUV multipatterning processes. When a circuit pattern is too fine or complex to print in one pass, it can be (put simply) divided into simpler parts and printed in several exposure and processing steps. Introducing High-NA means changing those established processes and materials while protecting yield (the proportion of chips on a wafer that emerge fully functional). For TSMC, waiting may therefore be perfectly rational. But the case for High-NA is just as understandable. Imec’s Geert Vandenberghe captures its central promise in two words: “single exposure.” In practical terms, that means printing a critical circuit pattern onto a chip layer in a single exposure rather than dividing it across multiple exposure and processing sequences. Fewer sequences can mean less production time, fewer opportunities for alignment errors, and fewer associated deposition and etching steps. But that advantage appears only when the surrounding materials, masks, measurement systems and process tools are ready. To illustrate the complexity: around 40 companies are collaborating through Imec (arguably the major development hub for the technology) to make that possible. The article also quotes ASML Chief Technology Officer Marco Pieters as putting average High-NA system availability at around 80%, compared with 93% for conventional EUV. For equipment of this cost, that difference matters enormously. .... SK Hynix's Nasdaq debut looked like a celebration of the AI memory boom.

I read it differently, and said so in Handelsblatt. The short version of my take: the memory shortage that briefly pushed SK Hynix above Samsung Electronics is also an opening Chinese suppliers are well placed to take. Or, as I put it to Martin Kölling and Joachim Hofer, „China wird sich die Chance nicht entgehen lassen." China won't let this chance slip. The debut was a real milestone. SK Hynix raised about 26.5 billion dollars through its US ADR offering, the largest ever by a foreign company. The shares priced at 149 dollars and closed their first day about 13% above the offer. Then came the reminder: this Monday the Seoul shares fell around 15%, their biggest one-day drop on record, on profit-taking and doubts about how much AI optimism is already priced in. This was never just a story about one Korean chipmaker. It was a stress test for every AI stock, after Nvidia had shed close to a trillion dollars in value over the prior two months. What makes SK Hynix so central is HBM, the stacked high-bandwidth memory that feeds AI accelerators. At Computex, Jensen Huang signed a sample of its latest HBM generation with the note "Please make more." That demand has rewritten the bottleneck: for two years it was compute, now memory has joined it. HBM production increasingly competes with commodity DRAM capacity, so even ordinary DDR5 is now scarce, and some suppliers are reporting gross margins approaching 80%. Scarcity this profitable is an invitation. When Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron hold roughly 90% of the DRAM market and prices keep climbing, major system companies begin evaluating alternative suppliers. Apple is reportedly evaluating DRAM from China's CXMT for selected products. And China is moving to build its own: DigiTimes reports plans involving Huawei, SwaySure and government-backed entities for a 12-inch DRAM fab in Shenzhen. Neither Huawei nor the other parties have confirmed the report, and export controls on advanced lithography will slow China's leading edge. The strategic trend is hard to ignore. SK Hynix's CEO, Kwak Noh-jung, expects next year to be the worst in the industry's history for supply, with demand outrunning capacity beyond 2030. The same shortage that supports today’s margins also strengthens the incentive to build alternative supply. Every year the West stays short gives China’s memory ecosystem more time to catch up. None of this suggests a collapse. It shows how today’s shortage is shaping tomorrow’s competitive landscape. Grateful to Martin Kölling and Joachim Hofer at Handelsblatt for including my perspective, alongside analysis from Techinsights and iM Securities. How do you see today’s memory shortage: another industry cycle, or the beginning of a structural realignment? 🔗

The technological gravity of automated driving is shifting east and to the US.

I told VDI nachrichten / ingenieur.de why that is not, in itself, a German weakness. The recent headlines about automated driving alliances in Germany prompted the conversation: where the future of German automated driving really sits, and how automotive technology now crosses over into physical AI far beyond the car. Two disciplines set the pace today: vertical integration of chips, software and vehicle, and the speed of learning cycles from fleet data. German manufacturers have answered by buying that competence through partnerships and stakes, not building all of it alone. That is a smart response, not a retreat. Many read those same headlines as failure and giving up on core competencies, yet German technology powers today the best-rated driver assistance systems in China. And Sino-German JV’s have tangible products for highly automated driving ready. From Chip to Cloud. (See by Marcus Hafkemeyer of CARIZON ) The real question is not whether you partner, but whether you keep system sovereignty over the vehicle while doing so. When a technology partner becomes a system-critical supplier, a new cluster risk replaces the old one. That risk points to something deeper, because the shift underneath is entrepreneurial, not technical. Software does not sell once, it sells across the whole lifecycle. So bills of materials and ramp-up curves now have to give way to update capability, platform scaling and recurring revenue. The industry needs people who run software like a business, not managers paid to stop things from going wrong. Robotics follows the same logic. German competence in mechanics, sensors, actuators and volume manufacturing is a real head start, but it is an entry ticket, not the jackpot. Here too, the winner integrates hardware and software and ships it in time. And one asset stays underrated: the data treasure German industry holds in its own factories. Perfecting these systems in production, where Germany still leads, could become the decisive advantage, the bridge from physical components to intelligent software. Full article (German) in comments. "Sounds like a plan."

Join me for a peek behind the curtain of China's national compute fabric strategy as it aims to become the leading AI superpower. WirtschaftsWoche this week reports with satellite imagery and on-the-ground investigation: the status of Beijing's country-scale AI infrastructure build-up. At a speed that is hard to grasp from Europe. Some context: The strategy is "Eastern Data, Western Compute". Data is generated in the eastern megacities. Compute happens in the west, where solar-generated electricity costs two cents per kilowatt-hour and the Gobi keeps servers cool. Some call it "the golden latitude": sunny and windy, with an abundance of cheap electricity, yet cold enough to save more than 50% on cooling energy. Reports say another €260 billion is being prepared over five years for this nationwide AI data center network. The current 15th Five-Year Plan (2026–2030) expands this into a broader "six networks" infrastructure framework: water network, new-type power grid, computing power network, next-generation communication network, urban underground pipeline network, and logistics network. What do the experts in the article say about China's strategy: Thomas Nuernberger of ebm-papst sees a "good enough" approach built for price leadership. Prof. Dr. Stefan Kesselheim at Forschungszentrum Jülich notes that "where costs fall, demand rises disproportionately." Volker A. Pfirsching of Arthur D. Little points to Huawei and Cambricon as viable domestic alternatives to Nvidia and AMD. Antonia Hmaidi at Mercator Institute for China Studies (MERICS) frames the stakes: for Beijing, leading the fourth industrial revolution is survival-critical. I could not agree more. My view in the piece sits at the intersection of technology, industrialization, and business models. In Europe, we watch for the next Chinese "wonder" model or "super-chip." China is pacing itself to catch up, yes. But the real story is industrial: a broad, cheap, good, and sovereign compute base that undercuts Western pricing by orders of magnitude. DeepSeek already proved the point. It's V4 Pro at $0.44/million input tokens versus OpenAI's $5.00; not just a discount. When companies face enormous cost pressure, this may be enough to trade AI sovereignty for competitiveness. Token efficiency debates aside, China may already hold the upper hand in terms of reasonable prices and good performance. There is also a broader pattern. China, and America as well, treat civil and defense needs as intertwined. Compute, AI, robotics, automotive, manufacturing. They cluster and progress together. Europe is organizing this differently at the moment. Thank you to Thomas Stölzel and Jörn Petring for connecting threads many of us have watched in isolation. Solar. Batteries. EVs. Now compute. The question is not whether China exports this capability. It is when Europe is ready. For your weekend: full article in the print edition, link in the comments.

$1.7 billion in 2025. $19.9 billion by 2035.

The passenger-car digital twin market is on a near-12x trajectory, and the reason is not better 3D rendering. The lazy view still treats the digital twin as a virtual prototype: build it, validate it, move on. That view is now obsolete. As cars become defined by software rather than physical properties, hardware-first validation is breaking down. The twin has stopped being an isolated engineering tool and become the connective tissue of the entire lifecycle: engineering, manufacturing, supply chain, and the vehicle in the field. I made this argument to Automotive News Europe this week, alongside other industry voices. Thanks to Lois Jones (Hoyal) for the thoughtful piece and for bringing these perspectives together. "We are simulating complex dynamic systems across the life cycle." The savings are real. Virtual-first development shortens cycles and cuts prototype cost. But the strategic value runs deeper. "The automated driving systems of today learn their skills mainly on the virtual roads of simulation systems." Without the twin, autonomy does not scale. Here is what most coverage misses: this is not a car story. It is a physical-AI story. The same loop that validates a software-defined vehicle validates a humanoid on a factory line, a robot in a warehouse, an autonomous system in a defence context. Automotive is simply where the discipline matured first. Cross-domain transfer, from automotive to robotics to aerospace, is the structural advantage now in play. Siemens made this concrete in last December with PAVE360, a system-level twin that lets a virtual car run years before the silicon exists. Credit to Kristian Kozole and the Siemens team. And (not only) on the manufacturing side, thanks to Karin Lehner and the Amazon Web Services (AWS) team: the e-Bike Smart Factory demo remains one of my favourite hands-on examples of a manufacturing twin running live, closing the loop from shop-floor sensors to simulation in real time. The technology is transversal. The advantage goes to whoever operationalizes it across sectors fastest. 👉

"Low power is the new superpower."

Lars Reger, CTO of NXP Semiconductors, said it on stage at the Global Semiconductor Alliance European Executive Forum in Munich just now. He was talking about edge AI and physical intelligence. He could have been talking about Europe's entire semiconductor strategy. The imec CEO Patrick Vandenameele asked in his session a question that could have been rhetorical: "Can Europe still play a role?" His answer was data, not optimism. Global private investment in AI to date: $471 billion in the US, $119 billion in China. Europe is fragmented into national slices. And the cost of inaction is compounding: Europe's projected 30-40 GW of data center capacity by 2035 could generate roughly €1.4 trillion in margins. Most of it will be captured outside the continent. But Vandenameele also laid out why the question is still open. Europe retains four assets that matter: research depth, market scale, industrial base, and talent. The challenge is not absence of capability. It is coordination. Europe needs to build compute demand and lead distributed AI — not by copying the US hyperscaler model, but by leveraging its strength in edge, automotive, and industrial systems where low power is indeed the superpower. Reger framed the engineering imperative precisely: "Engineering. Not hype." Four pillars: responsible engineering, efficient scalable edge AI, reliability and integrity, modular and adaptable architectures. No manifestos. Build the foundation. Capgemini is contributing directly. Julian van Velzen, CTIO of our Quantum Lab, will join the panel on scaling quantum semiconductors from lab to fab. Theo van Roekel presents Tracces and a new study on trusted access and remote connectivity in semiconductor manufacturing — the security layer that industrial AI at scale actually requires. Three technology transitions are converging here: AI reshaping chip design and demand, physical AI moving semiconductors from component to platform, and quantum moving from lab to fab. Europe has the research base, the market, the talent, and now the policy framework with Chips Act 2.0. The window is open.
