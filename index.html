Semiconductors, software-defined products, physical AI and China — and what they actually mean for the industries betting on them.

Most technology stories get told twice: once as the headline, and once as what actually has to work for the headline to be true. I spend my time on the second one — the economics, the engineering stack, the timing. Much of that view was formed in Beijing, where I lived and worked for many years. These days you can hear from me here, on LinkedIn, or in the major media outlets that call when they need an industry read.

About me

I'm VP Technology & Innovation at Capgemini Engineering, leading the technology and innovation portfolio across Northern & Central Europe, and Managing Director of Microsys Technologies. The work sits where engineering meets economics: what a technology can do, when it becomes worth using, and what that means for the companies whose plans depend on the answer.

I learned that question the slow way, and largely in China. Beijing was home for a long stretch, and the job there was to build AVL's customer service organisation from the ground up, while the country turned into the world's most important automotive market. It was a privilege to experience the Chinese mobility industry from the inside — to contribute to it, and perhaps to shape a little of it. You don't watch a transformation like that from the outside and come away understanding it the same way.

Read moreShow less

China has stayed with me ever since — not only as part of my working life, but as a genuine personal interest. It is one of the forces shaping how the world works today and how it will work tomorrow, and that is not something you follow at arm's length. It is also why I still find the contrast between Europe, China and North America more instructive than any one of them on its own.

It's also why I try to be precise. Behind a utilisation figure or a restructuring headline are engineers, plants, families and often generations of industrial know-how. Being exact about the numbers and honest about what they mean is the least one owes them — and more useful, anyway, than being either alarmist or reassuring.

I started out in research, on an Austrian space mission project at the University of Innsbruck. Today I chair Capgemini's Global Ground Mobility Strategy Board, and a good part of my week goes into writing and speaking about where these technologies are actually heading.

The Paris Motor Show is almost here. Beautiful new models will stir emotions and remind us why we love cars.

Design and driving pleasure are part of that fascination. Increasingly, so is intelligence: a car that understands what we ask and makes everyday journeys easier. And intelligence works both ways. AI can enrich the driver’s experience. It can also help manufacturers develop better products, validate them faster and build them more efficiently. A more desirable car and a more competitive business belong in the same conversation. At Capgemini, we will show this in Paris with demonstrations of how AI supports the engineering of hardware and software, manufacturing, and the customer experience. The part closest to my own work goes right down to the silicon. Bringing AI into the vehicle means deciding today which functions should run locally, how much computing power and memory they need, and who can support them over the vehicle’s life. Choosing a chip also means choosing a software ecosystem. That shapes what the car can do and how much room there is to improve it later. Handelsblatt recently looked at how BMW and Mercedes-Benz approach these choices, and I was glad to contribute. A summary credited to Taiwan’s International Trade Administration has since appeared on the leading Taiwanese financial portal MoneyDJ. It includes a point I raised: the AI boom intensifies carmakers’ competition with other technology sectors for high-performance computing and memory chip capacity. When supply is that contested, how do you get from now to next? You can adopt a new high-performance platform from one of the big vendors, with the lock-in that may come with it. You can design your own silicon-and we are happy to help! Or you can find a smart way to add intelligent acceleration to the architecture you already have. That third path is why I am watching companies like SiMa.ai, with its leaders Krishna Rangasayee and Harald (Harry) Kroeger. Their low-power Modalix chip is built to run AI models efficiently inside the vehicle. That is local intelligence a driver would actually notice: an assistant that still answers in a tunnel. Integration and validation still take real work, but there is scope to build on what already works. For me, the exciting engineering challenge is making intelligence useful, dependable and affordable enough to bring more joy to everyday driving. The green giant has company. And the race does not stop at the data centre.

Today’s Handelsblatt looks at Google’s growing challenge to Nvidia. Many thanks to Joachim Hofer and Philipp Alvares de Souza Soares for the conversation. As I said in the article, customers are looking hard for alternatives, while Nvidia’s ecosystem remains “unfassbar stark” (incredibly strong). Behind the chip race lies an enormous wave of investment, fuelled by expectations of what AI could do. The commercial test is whether useful applications turn those expectations into sustained demand. Personal assistants and agentic workflows show one way that could happen: researching, planning and acting on our behalf can involve a whole sequence of model calls. If these services earn a lasting place in everyday work and life, the cost of completing tasks reliably becomes a recurring business concern. And a reason to explore different compute options. Google and Amazon are expanding their own silicon strategies, while Huawei and Alibaba are pushing ahead in China, where supply security and regulatory requirements also shape technology choices. Some carmakers are becoming chip designers themselves: Tesla and Rivian tailor silicon to their autonomy workloads. Specialists such as Horizon Robotics supply dedicated chips for assisted driving. And yes, Huawei shows up here, too, through its automotive computing platforms. For industrial AI, the long game also extends to the vehicle, the machine and the robot. At the edge, the requirements become very tangible: watts, heat, milliseconds, safety and years of reliable operation. Providers such as Axelera AI offer European alternatives for suitable inference workloads. With Europe’s industrial base and engineering knowledge, that is an opportunity we should take seriously. Still, chip performance, availability and price are only one side of the coin. The software ecosystem can be decisive, alongside the engineering effort required to run models efficiently across different hardware. Savings on the chip can disappear into migration costs, poor utilization or downtime. In the end, the application has to pay for itself. That is where our work at Capgemini becomes very concrete: understanding where AI makes sense in a process or product, and bringing together the engineering needed to make it work. This means connecting silicon and embedded software with data, models, cloud and edge infrastructure, through to embodied AI that can operate safely in the physical world. Owning the chip can make sense when the workload, volumes and lifecycle justify it. What ultimately counts is how reliably and economically the complete system delivers value for the customer. We talk a lot about software-defined vehicles.

Much less about the silicon that decides what they can do, and who controls it. Thanks to Handelsblatt, Joachim Hofer, Michael Scheppe and Felix Stippler for putting this topic on the digital front page. I'm glad to have contributed alongside BMW's Joachim Post, Mercedes-Benz's Joerg Burzer and former BMW board member Pieter Nota. The direction is clear: high-performance vehicle computers. A first step brings safety-critical ADAS and non-critical infotainment into a single housing; deeper integration puts them on a single chip, with strict safety isolation. Mercedes-Benz is also pursuing purpose-built chips. Suppliers such as NVIDIA and Qualcomm bring hardware, software and development ecosystems. That accelerates launches while creating dependencies on roadmaps, licensing and revenue-sharing models. Intel's withdrawal from the automotive architecture market shows how supplier strategies can change. Tier-1s face pressure, but also shape this transition. In China, Bosch combines system expertise with ADAS platforms from NVIDIA and Horizon Robotics. Partners and competitors at the same time. Huawei takes this further: chips, software, vehicle technology and brands developed with OEM partners. Its automotive business remains centered on China, but parts of that ecosystem are already moving abroad. I expect that reach to deepen (partner vehicles, alliances). The badge on the bonnet will not necessarily tell us whose technology defines the car. OEMs must also navigate memory shortages, pressure on packaging substrates and diverging US–China ecosystems. American platforms remain influential; Chinese platforms gain ground in China. The split is growing, but incomplete. One angle I would have liked to explore further: own silicon. Tesla has shown the custom-silicon model. XPeng's Turing chips now power Volkswagen's ID. UNYX 08 in China, and XPeng is reportedly seeking technology customers beyond VW. Silicon and software become a second business alongside selling cars. Custom silicon increases design control. Manufacturing, packaging and memory dependencies remain. For European carmakers, advanced-node custom silicon can still look out of reach. Cost, scale and manufacturing access are real hurdles. But shared chiplets offer a way forward. At Capgemini, this is what Francois Babin, Bogdan Bižić, Rahul Arya and I work on. Our chiplet initiative aims to improve European access to custom advanced-node chips for automotive, defence and other critical applications. Reusing building blocks across sectors can spread investment while preserving differentiation. For me, every SDV strategy is also a semiconductor strategy. Europe needs credible choices about which foundations it owns, shares and sources. 👉

Robotic systems must be able to learn. Their safety has to hold as they change.

That is just one takeaway from today’s discussions at The Autonomous Main Event in Vienna’s magnificent Hofburg. The Autonomous brings industry leaders together to develop common foundations for safe and trusted autonomy across mobility and robotics. Lars Reger of NXP again made the case for a layered safety architecture with clearly structured control levels. He questioned whether a purely centralized high-performance computing approach can deliver what trusted autonomy requires. The panel featuring Mathias Pillin of Bosch highlighted what robotics can learn from the automotive industry: system architectures and frameworks for learning and validation. I agree. Systems must be capable, safe, and reliable while learning and adapting. Every improvement needs evidence that it maintains safety. At Capgemini, we work to build this chain of trust from chip to cloud, drawing on engineering experience across aerospace, space, medical technology, automotive, energy, and other critical industries. My colleague Dr. Ulrich Wurstbauer and I will explore related questions on collaboration in the auto/robot sector at the upcoming ELIV - Electronics in Vehicles. The international reach of The Autonomous is growing, too. Qualcomm joined the newly established association as a member. Contributions from Horizon Robotics, Sonatus, Wayve and Samsung underline that breadth, alongside the experience of Young Sohn and Hermann Hauser in building and backing technology businesses. Especially encouraging to see European robotics champions represented by speakers such as Péter Fankhauser of ANYbotics. For me, this signals confidence in the technology and in Europe’s ability to help shape the robotics revolution ahead. A big thank you to Lars Reger for the invitation, and to Ricky Hudi, Georg Kopetz, The Autonomous team, and the sponsors. A wonderful setting to learn, challenge assumptions, and reconnect. Trusted autonomy means continuing to improve systems while maintaining confidence in their behavior.

Some things you simply cannot invent.

This morning I walked into IAA TRANSPORTATION in Hannover and they played Chinese pop music at the ticket check! It felt like the fair had summarized itself: Chinese players have never been stronger. China is no longer just bringing trucks and vans to Europe. It is building the ecosystem around them. And the mandarin pop songs were the soundtrack of change. Foton has established parts distribution in Germany. MAXUS has moved its German import business directly under SAIC Motor Deutschland. Huawei is entering charging infrastructure. Chinese truck makers are also localizing production in Europe. Sinotruk has started truck assembly at Steyr Automotive, while SuperPanther is now producing its electric eTopas 600 there. The historical symmetry is hard to miss. In 1983, Steyr 91 truck technology went to China. More than forty years later, Chinese trucks are being built back in Steyr. And people are watching. European executives were following the Chinese presentations very closely. Drivers I spoke with after testing Chinese battery trucks were generally positive, particularly about drivability and the cab experience. Then there is Tesla. The Semi is not remarkable simply because it is electric. Many trucks here are. What stood out to me sitting inside was the usability: central seating, excellent visibility, novel door concept, integrated camera views and a remarkably clean interface. It feels like someone reconsidered the truck as a product. But Europe has certainly not given up. Quite the opposite. MAN is pushing the next eTGX toward 720 km range and 750 kW charging. Mercedes-Benz Trucks is expanding the eActros family with the new Lowliner. Scania’s electric offering is highly visible on the show floor. Hydrogen get’s pushed. And suppliers such as Bosch and ZF continue to push the e-drive, E/E and software architecture underneath all of this forward. The same is true for SDV. Less conversation, more action, as the King would say. The discussion is becoming much more concrete: scalable E/E architectures, central compute, fleet software, energy management and charging integration. Less PowerPoint. More implementation. Automated driving feels similar. Less grand promise, more real applications in last-mile delivery, municipal vehicles and constrained duty cycles. My takeaway from Hannover is therefore quite simple: Europe does not primarily have a technology problem. It has a competitiveness problem. Speed. Cost. Usability. Service. Uptime. And ultimately TCO. Europe still has formidable strengths in engineering, service networks and fleet integration. But technology alone will not decide this race. Competitiveness will. And tonight I’m looking forward to the Stars of the Year ceremony, presented by Capgemini Sebastian Tschödrich , Jens Sulek . A fitting end to a day full of strong products, serious competition and plenty to think about.

Up to 30% cheaper. How should Europe’s truck industry respond?

That is the question behind the headline of my interview with Nicole Holzer for firmenauto. Chinese electric truck entrants are targeting purchase prices substantially below European offerings. These are price ambitions, not a confirmed discount for every model. But the challenge goes beyond price. Consider BYD’s new ETT 44 truck: a claimed 20-minute charge from 20% to 80% using megawatt charging, plus a standard battery warranty of ten years or 1.2 million kilometers. Charging, energy management and financing accompany the vehicle. Europe’s manufacturers have upped their game, too. MAN’s battery-electric eTGX, Daimler Truck’s expanding Mercedes-Benz eActros family and Volvo’s new heavy electric range, just named International Truck of the Year 2027, demonstrate substantial capability. Europe has compelling products. The task is to make them compelling investments. So, what changes that calculation for fleet owners? Switzerland offers a concrete example. Its current heavy-vehicle charge exemption saves a 40-tonne electric truck roughly 96,000 Swiss francs per 100,000 domestic kilometres compared with a modern Euro VI diesel. MAN’s “Beyond Diesel” report likewise identifies potential five-year ownership cost savings of up to 13% compared with diesel, with suitable depot charging and energy arrangements. Electric transport can make commercial sense when the conditions fit. Making those conditions available more widely requires action. The EU targets truck charging every 60 km on core European transport routes by 2030. Yet July’s count was just 730 public charging points above 350 kilowatts dedicated to heavy vehicles across the EU, according to Germany’s automotive industry association. Europe should seize this industrial opportunity: competitive vehicle costs and financing, dependable service, affordable electricity and charging that fits the operation. Together, these help customers invest and manufacturers build scale. To put the opportunity in perspective: electric trucks accounted for 28% of new heavy freight truck sales in China in 2025, compared with around 1.5% in Europe, according to the International Energy Agency. Different markets, certainly. But a clear indication of how far electric trucking can progress. Europe has strong foundations to build on. Thank you, Nicole, for the conversation. What does China's robotics momentum mean for Europe's industrial position?

My previous posts this week captured what was visible at the World Robot Conference in Beijing. My recent interview with China.Table addresses that more consequential question. Having been a "Beijinger" myself for many years, I already have a baseline for how quickly China can organize an industry. Even against that baseline, the pace of embodied AI stands out. The timing of Europe keeping pace is relevant. The German federal government has now named industrial AI a strategic priority. In the same edition of China.Table that reported this shift, my interview sets out what that priority requires in practice. The useful way to read the humanoid race is in four layers: 1) The brain: the chips. Nvidia remains highly present for AI in China. 2) The body: sensors, gears, motors, MCUs. China has built a remarkably vital supplier ecosystem in very little time. European companies remain strong in this layer, with capabilities from Stuttgart, Munich, Hamburg, Dresden, Herzogenaurach and Bruchsal. But competition is intensifying. 3) The hands: whoever delivers many degrees of freedom and reliable touch at low cost will set the pace. Good robotic hands still cost several thousand euros; simplified Chinese models are available wholesale from around €500. 4) The mind: the models that determine whether a machine merely follows a program or understands and interacts with its surroundings. This is where the decisive race is taking place. These four layers do not develop in isolation, and the data layer is the most overlooked. China is also building the training-data infrastructure and the links between policy, research, capital, and industry that enable them to scale together. Europe is strong in the body and in essential parts of the brain. Its biggest gap is in the mind. Two things will decide the next five years: who integrates all four layers into one functioning ecosystem, and who wins on cost. A robot that works but is too expensive will remain a "showpony." The larger opportunity may lie beyond humanoids. Models developed for them can also improve the industrial robots already operating in factories by the million. I expect the economic return to arrive there first, sooner than the spectacle suggests. Europe does not need to reproduce China's model. It needs to more quickly connect its component strengths, installed industrial base, and AI capabilities. This is also why embodied AI matters to our work at Capgemini: bringing consulting, technology and engineering together to translate AI into products, services and measurable industrial value. Thank you, Amelie Richter, for a precise conversation and the space to develop the argument. The full interview appeared in China.Table on Tuesday, the summary in CEO.Table on Wednesday. 👉

Where Physical AI and robotics go next is being shaped right now!

In a few days, I travel to the World Robot Conference in Beijing to see for myself where the research is heading. What Physical AI and humanoid robotics mean today is the other half of the question, and that one we looked at in an IT-BUSINESS cover story together with KUKA and Dassault Systèmes. I was glad to share those pages with two partners we work with closely. Perception was never really the bottleneck. Robots have carried sensors for years, and what has changed is that AI now allows a machine to interpret an unstructured environment, to decide within the scope it has been given, and to adapt while it works. Instructions are moving from programmed motion paths towards natural language. This capability does not arrive as a replacement, and I appreciate that Christoph Schell, CEO of KUKA Group, said so plainly: Automation 1.0 remains the backbone, while Automation 2.0 adds new flexibility. The new layer goes precisely where rigidity has been the constraint. Getting that layer into a plant is where the real work begins. Jeroen Buring, Senior Director Eurocentral at Dassault Systèmes, points to the combination of Virtual Twins, Industrial AI and Physical AI. Before a robot moves on a line, it should move in a virtual twin, because simulation is by far the cheaper place to fail. Behind this sits an infrastructure bill: data volumes rise sharply, twins need compute, and mobile robots need wireless coverage most plants do not have. IT and OT stop being separate worlds. Ambition is running ahead of deployment. In the Capgemini Research Institute survey of 1,678 executives across 15 industries, 60 percent expect Physical AI to open up robotics in settings where it was previously impractical. The same executives put the average timeline for scaling humanoids at around seven years, and six in ten name public acceptance as a critical obstacle. Both answers from the same people! What I see in our own projects is a good deal less spectacular. The near-term entry point lies in the evolution of familiar form factors: automated transport systems, robotic arms, collaborative semi-stationary systems. Humanoids are the premium class, and they will matter, but most of them are still too expensive, too clumsy and too unreliable for a production line. For European industry, the decisive figure is not the demo. It is the speed of the step from pilot to broad productive use. That step is where we come in with our clients: analyzing the process before the robot, finding the use cases that actually pay, selecting the right technology, and carrying it through training and implementation until it runs in production. The title of our research report puts it about right. Make it real. In Beijing, I will look at hardware maturity, unit economics, and how much has moved from demo to repeatable work. "Human-Robot Symbiosis, Production Demand Convergence" is the official theme of the World Robot Conference…

The event is really three in one. It is a scientific conference, it is a B2B sales show, and it is a Volksfest for the broader public. What is remarkable is not only the strategic focus this country puts into robotics, or how strongly enterprises have picked it up, but how positively the wider society is looking at all of it. The floors are filled to the brim, and when the CEOs of the robot startups give a keynote, it is not much short of a pop event. I spent the week there, and had the pleasure of speaking live to Antje Erhard of tagesschau24 from Beijing. The show is dominated by humanoids, great show pieces that demonstrate real mechanical and control excellence. On that evidence alone, the future of universal mechanical helpers looks imminent. What is less obvious is that the next play fields, the ones that turn those promises into products, are better robot hands and improved AI models, and Chinese companies are competing vigorously in both. Further insight sits in the programme rather than in the halls. There is a dedicated procurement day, and for the first time a pavilion where 49 central state-owned enterprises present twelve joint application scenarios. China is not only building supply here, it is organising demand. This is not a Chinese and American game alone. In my tagesschau24 interview I pointed out that sensors from Stuttgart, chips from Munich and Hamburg or components from Herzogenaurach are part of this, often perceived Chinese, robot revolution, and the floor bore that out, with Infineon (Thomas Schneid ) and Schaeffler (Roberto Henkel ) both exhibiting in the Collaboration Pavilion, a Schaeffler dexterous hand beside a Schaeffler HPC for humanoids. It was just as good to see German science placed prominently in the programme, with Prof Alois Knoll (TU Munich), Prof Wolfram Burgard (UT Nuremberg), Prof Michael Dowling (Regensburg) and Prof. Dr. Ferdinand Dudenhoeffer among the speakers. Europe has a base. What we need now is to accelerate. That acceleration will happen against markets that are separating. When the FCC added advanced robotic devices to its Covered List in July, the test turned on where a device is produced, not on who produces it, which puts European production on the same side of that line as China's. For enterprises, besides the buzz, the often promising demonstrations and the fear of missing out, the real work is to clearly identify the opportunities and the limits of this technology, and the use cases that really create value. Study

AI in the Automotive Development Process: From Siloed Experiments to Industrialized Intelligence

Study by Capgemini, TUM and ASIMI, with Elmar Frickenstein, Alois Knoll, Uwe Michael, Steffen Krause and Charlotte Link · 2026

Every organization we asked already uses AI in engineering. Almost none scales it end to end. We put that question to senior leaders across OEMs, software Tier-1s, integrators, hyperscalers and research. Their answer: the wall is not the model, it is the organization. Only 20–30% of AI pilots reach production; those who design industrialization in from day one report more than 50%. Buy the model, own the context.

Unveiled at ELIV 2026. Download link on capgemini.com coming soon.

Things I build

Open source

openclaw-meshcore

OpenClaw channel plugin for MeshCore · TypeScript · MIT · 2026

Connects an OpenClaw AI agent to a MeshCore LoRa mesh, the off-grid radio network that runs without internet or cell towers. Direct messages work both ways. Public channels are receive-only, enforced in code, because airtime on a mesh is a shared commons. Safe by default: new contacts need explicit approval, and radio settings stay read-only.

View on GitHub →
Open source · Fork

openclaw-meshtastic

OpenClaw channel plugin for Meshtastic · TypeScript · 2026

My fork of Sergey Krashevich's plugin, which connects OpenClaw to a Meshtastic LoRa mesh over HTTP, TCP or serial. OpenClaw 2026.6.5 replaced the API that channel plugins use to receive messages. I migrated the plugin to the new adapter pattern without changing its behavior, fixed its delivery error logging along the way, and offered the change back upstream.

What I'm reading

Books that changed how I think about this. A short list, on purpose.

Technology Strategy

Chip War

by Chris Miller

The book I hand people who ask why chips keep turning up in foreign policy. Miller's case is that where you can make something decides who holds the leverage.

Details →
Technology Strategy

The Innovator's Dilemma

by Clayton M. Christensen

I keep coming back to this one. Christensen's point is that good companies fail not by doing the wrong things, but by doing the right ones for the customers they already have.

Details →
China

AI Superpowers

by Kai-Fu Lee

Lee's argument that implementation beats invention has aged well — and from where I sat in Beijing, the chapters on execution speed read less like prediction than description.

Details →
China

The China Questions

by Jennifer Rudolph & Michael Szonyi (eds.)

Not one argument but many — China scholars disagreeing with each other in a single volume. Useful precisely because it refuses to reduce the country to one storyline.

Details →
China

Wenn China angreift

by Andreas Fulda

A 2027 Taiwan scenario followed all the way through to its economic consequences. The chapters on German exposure are the ones I'd give anyone who thinks this is a distant problem.

Details →
AI & Industries

Prediction Machines

by Ajay Agrawal, Joshua Gans & Avi Goldfarb

The most useful reframing I know: AI as a fall in the cost of prediction. See it that way and which tasks it changes — and which it doesn't — stops being guesswork.

Details →
AI & Industries

Human + Machine

by Paul R. Daugherty & H. James Wilson

The 'missing middle' — the work that only exists when people and machines do it together. It's the part of the automation debate that usually gets skipped.

Details →
AI & Us

A World Appears

by Michael Pollan

Pollan spends the whole book asking what consciousness actually is and never pretends to land it. That patience is the point — we are busy building machines that act intelligent without knowing what experiencing anything even means.
